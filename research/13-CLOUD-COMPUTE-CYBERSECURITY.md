# Algeria Cloud, Compute and Cybersecurity Landscape

**Snapshot:** 1 November 2026  
**Status:** emerging infrastructure and managed-services lane; market sizing not yet justified.

## 1. Executive assessment

The local infrastructure market is moving beyond conventional web hosting. Current signals include:
- a regulator-defined general-authorization route for cloud hosting/storage;
- an ARPCE directory naming 20 cloud-service operators, with a dated status caveat;
- public and private cloud offers, including local GPU-as-a-Service claims;
- developer-oriented sovereign cloud initiatives;
- a cloud-virtualization partnership;
- a capital raise by AYRADE intended to support data-center expansion;
- institutional procurement notices for GPU compute, SOC equipment, backup/restore and managed cybersecurity.

This is evidence of ecosystem formation and institutional demand signals. It does **not** establish a profitable market gap, market-wide spending, or a viable new infrastructure provider.

The likely entry points to investigate are value-added services around existing infrastructure: application migration, managed AI workloads, GPU capacity orchestration, security operations, backup/recovery, data governance, cloud cost/performance engineering and sector-specific integration. A new data-center or general-purpose cloud infrastructure build is not the default recommendation.

## 2. Regulatory and market structure

ARPCE classifies hosting and storage in cloud computing under the general-authorization regime. Its published application procedure calls for:
- technical and commercial service description;
- infrastructure architecture and connection method;
- equipment, storage capacity and associated software;
- backup-system description and dedicated capacity;
- data-security systems;
- description of hosting premises, including area, power supply, air conditioning and physical security;
- legal, financial and company documentation.

The page lists 20 entities in its cloud-services operator directory, including Algeria Telecom, AYRADE, CERIST, DATAGIX, Electronic Business Services, ISSAL NET, Netbeopen Prime and others. Four entries carry an asterisk; ARPCE's note says those licence holders' regularization applications were being examined. The page displays 11 March 2025 as its update date. It is therefore a useful dated provider map, not a definitive current licensing register as of November 2026. Confirm a provider's current authorization directly before relying on its status. [S37]

The scope of the regime for a software-only SaaS provider using another operator's infrastructure, versus a company that itself provides cloud hosting/storage to third parties, requires legal/regulatory interpretation. Do not infer the answer from product labels alone.

## 3. Provider and platform map

### AYRADE

The Algiers Stock Exchange's official company page describes AYRADE as an IT and digital-services company working in cloud computing, cybersecurity, system integration, ERP/BI and consulting. The page states cloud-service authorization in 2024 and reports a June 2026 public offering of 1,250,000 new shares at 800 DZD each, a gross amount of 1 billion DZD, intended to finance data-center expansion and digital projects. This is a disclosed financing plan and provider profile; actual capex completion, data-center capacity and service performance remain to be checked. [S41]

### ICOSNET

ICOSNET markets web hosting, VPS, cloud instances and a public-cloud offer. Its website advertises entry pricing of 2,800 DZD/month or 4.5 DZD/hour, and dedicated NVIDIA GPU-as-a-Service for AI, machine learning and 3D use. The company describes two data centers in Algeria, in Algiers and Oran. All capacity, uptime, GPU availability, technical specification and pricing are provider claims until tested and quoted. [S38]

**Regulatory cross-check:** ICOSNET appears as an asterisked entry in the ARPCE cloud-operator directory described above. Because the directory's displayed update date is March 2025, this is a discrepancy to clarify—not a basis to assert current non-compliance. [S37]

### AventureCloudz — Djezzy, Algeria Venture and Taubyte

Djezzy announced a partnership with the public accelerator Algeria Venture and Taubyte on 29 April 2026 to launch AventureCloudz, a full-stack AI/developer platform hosted on the Djezzy Cloud marketplace. APS also reported the platform's launch on 30 April 2026. [S39]

The AventureCloudz site describes a developer cloud with Git-based deployment, custom domains, TLS, serverless functions, databases, object storage, messaging, websites and AI workflows. The site displays example applications and says the platform is live, while some parts of its published service list are described as planned. Public pricing, production SLA, independent performance, capacity, security certification and actual paying-customer count are not established by the pages reviewed. [S40]

### Djezzy and Armonika

On 24 August 2026, Djezzy announced a partnership with Armonika under Algeria Venture to develop sovereign-cloud and infrastructure-virtualization solutions. Djezzy describes Armonika's software as including virtualization, orchestration, cloud services and automation. This is a strategic partnership announcement, not proof that a complete production service has been delivered or commercially adopted. [S45]

### CERIST Cloud Services and public research infrastructure

CERIST appears in ARPCE's cloud operator directory and states on its Cloud Services site that it is authorized by ARPCE to provide cloud hosting/storage. Its public feature page advertises:
- VPS configurations from 1 vCPU / 2 GB RAM / 20 GB disk through 6 vCPU / 12 GB RAM / 120 GB disk, with custom configurations;
- block storage, NFS/SMB shared storage and Swift/S3-compatible object storage;
- backup and snapshots;
- virtual networks and routers, IPAM/DHCP/DNS, VPN, firewall-as-a-service and L4/L7 load balancing;
- backup/restore tooling compatible with multiple hypervisors and virtualized environments.

This is a substantial local IaaS baseline, not a narrow compute-only offer. Pricing, throughput, contractual SLA, support response, capacity, data-location attestations and production performance were not established by the feature page. Treat the capabilities as provider-published until tested. [S47]

CERIST's 2026 procurement notices also provide institutional demand signals:
- GPU workstation and GPU-card acquisition for data analysis; one consultation specified compatibility with a NVIDIA Blackwell Workstation 96 GB-class card;
- a SOC video-wall procurement;
- backup/restore software, installation, three-year publisher support and training;
- a managed cybersecurity service for 12 months, covering audit, continuous monitoring and incident response on infrastructure including OpenStack virtual machines and firewall/WAF components;
- managed anti-DDoS service selection.

These are procurement consultations/notices, not proof that each contract was awarded, delivered or renewed. They demonstrate one research institution's concrete purchasing requirements, not national market size. [S42–S44]


### eBS — hosting, managed operations and data-center engineering

eBS advertises hosting, virtual/physical servers, monitoring, maintenance, server security, backup and managed services. It describes its data center at the Sidi Abdellah Cyber Park as 85 m², with a 35 m² production room and a 50 m² extension room, and identifies the facility as Tier III. It also markets data-center design, construction, fit-out, engineering and maintenance. These are company-published claims; independent certification, current operating capacity, power/redundancy design, SLA and utilization remain unverified. Some company pages show older copyright dates, so current product/service status should be confirmed directly. [S48–S49]


## 4. Cybersecurity policy and institutional operating model

Presidential Decree 26-07 of 7 January 2026 requires public institutions, administrations and public bodies to establish a structure responsible for information-system security and personal-data protection, independent of the IT department and attached to the head of the organization. Its duties include security policy, risk mapping and remediation, security/cyber-resilience solutions, audits, continuous monitoring, incident reporting, personal-data compliance coordination and staff awareness/training.

The decree's functional relationships are especially relevant to procurement: the security/data-protection structure coordinates with the public-procurement function to integrate information-security and confidentiality clauses where subcontracting is used. The decree also specifies coordination with the national information-systems security agency and the national personal-data authority. [S36]

This creates a clearer institutional problem owner and procurement interface. It does not mean every public body has fully staffed the structure, that budgets are available, or that outside vendors are automatically eligible.

The Ministry of National Defence announced publication of the National Information Systems Security Strategy 2025–2029 on 3 March 2026. Its existence is confirmed; details should be read from the published strategy itself, not inferred from the decree approving it. [S46]

## 5. Commercial opportunity hypotheses

| Hypothesis | Potential buyer | Possible offer | Main barrier / test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Managed cybersecurity and compliance operations | Public bodies, research institutions, regulated organizations, larger enterprises | Audit readiness, risk register, monitoring, incident response, hardening, evidence reports | Required certifications, trust, staffing, references, procurement access and 24/7 operating cost |
| Cloud migration and managed operations | SMEs, software vendors, public bodies, research/education | Migration, backup, observability, disaster recovery, performance and cost management | Incumbent provider capabilities, customer data, service-level obligations, support economics |
| GPU workload access and orchestration | AI startups, universities, labs, engineering firms | Inference endpoints, job scheduling, model serving, usage monitoring, capacity brokering | Actual GPU supply, utilization, price, import/repair constraints, network and power |
| AI deployment on local infrastructure | Organizations with local hosting or data-control needs | Private inference, retrieval, workflow automation, model evaluation, deployment and support | Workload demand, accelerator economics, security and support |
| Data governance and security implementation | Public bodies and large organizations | Data inventory/catalogue, data-flow maps, access controls, audit logs, privacy/security evidence | Legal scope, public procurement, existing integrators, need for legal/security credentials |
| Backup, resilience and recovery | Institutions and businesses with critical systems | Immutable backup, recovery testing, RPO/RTO design, managed recovery | Price sensitivity, incumbent service, proof of recovery capability and liability |

These are research hypotheses, not product recommendations.

## 6. Competitive pressure and strategic implications

The ecosystem now contains:
- infrastructure and cloud operators;
- local public and private data centers;
- telco-backed developer clouds;
- local virtualization and orchestration software;
- IT integrators and cybersecurity firms;
- research institutions with their own infrastructure and technical procurement.

Consequently:
1. Do not build another low-cost VPS or generic app-hosting product without a differentiated distribution or infrastructure advantage.
2. Do not assume GPU-as-a-Service is an empty category; Icosnet already markets the offer and institutional GPU procurement is visible.
3. Treat AventureCloudz and Armonika as emerging local competitors/partners whose actual production capabilities need hands-on testing.
4. Compare total cost per successful workload, including support, bandwidth, storage, backup, security, egress and compliance—not only CPU/GPU hourly price.
5. Consider partnerships or workload/software layers before capital-intensive infrastructure.
6. Investigate public tender eligibility, references, security qualifications, subcontracting clauses and service obligations before selecting public-sector GTM.

## 7. Required next tests

1. Verify the current ARPCE authorization status and service scope of named cloud operators, especially where the directory carries a regularization marker.
2. Obtain actual technical specifications and quotes from AYRADE, ICOSNET and AventureCloudz; test comparable CPU, storage, network, backup and GPU workloads.
3. Inspect production availability, API/SDK, observability, IAM, private networking, S3 compatibility, database support, backup and restore, data-location attestations and contractual SLAs.
4. Review CERIST tender specifications and results/awards, not just notices. Track later procurement cycles for recurring spend.
5. Map the cybersecurity vendor ecosystem and required credentials; separate product resale, implementation, audit and managed operations.
6. Identify the buyer, budget owner and current failure cost for each workload; do not infer enterprise spend from provider marketing.
7. Build a unit-economics model before considering GPU or data-center capex.

## 8. Current decision

**Continue the cloud, compute and cybersecurity research lane. Do not enter the commodity hosting market or purchase GPU infrastructure yet.** The evidence supports a growing ecosystem and real institutional procurement requirements, but the competitive field, licensing, utilization economics and buyer budgets remain unresolved.
