# Sector Landscape: Agriculture, Healthcare and Manufacturing in Algeria

**Snapshot:** 1 November 2026  
**Status:** first-pass sector maps; no market-size estimate or product commitment.

## 1. Executive assessment

These sectors are not at the same stage of digital development.

- **Agriculture:** a national information system has launched with a limited first module, while multiple agritech products, support programmes and opportunity concepts target irrigation, crop advice, drones, cold chain, traceability and market access. Technical and entrepreneurial activity is visible; farmer adoption, recurring budgets, unit economics and deployment scale are not established.
- **Healthcare:** the Ministry has set 15 November 2026 as the deadline for broad digitalization of university hospital centres (CHUs), reports more than 2,355 health structures connected to fibre, and says telemedicine/teleradiology have been introduced. That is a major public implementation signal, but fibre connection is not proof of a live electronic health record, data interoperability or completed adoption. A private medical-software market already offers clinic, hospital, pharmacy and practice-management systems.
- **Manufacturing:** studies describe gradual digital adoption, with capital cost and digital skills among barriers. One study surveyed 47 firms out of 230 approached; another surveyed 102 subcontracting manufacturers. A single-company SPEEDWIN case shows a common multi-system setup—production, accounting/sales and marketing tools—rather than a national pattern.

**Implication:** all three sectors merit continued research, but the opportunity likely lies in specific implementation and workflow gaps—not in building another broad platform. Need field workflow observation, current competitor testing, procurement/award records, implementation status, and segment-specific economics.

## 2. Agriculture and food systems

### 2.1 Public-sector information infrastructure

The National Agricultural Information System (Système national d'information agricole, SNAI) was officially launched on 29 June 2026. APS describes its purpose as providing reliable, updated agricultural data to support governance and central/local decision-making. [S67]

The first released module focuses on the harvest and threshing campaign. Ministry material describes daily field-data entry and monitoring, harvested area by region/crop, production, and operational issues such as shortages of combine harvesters. It also addresses grain producers without an agricultural card being able to market and deposit harvest at OAIC collection centres under the cited arrangement. Other proposed modules are to be rolled out progressively, including:
- national agricultural register;
- agricultural land register and land-use/fertility characterization;
- livestock, vaccination and animal identification;
- agricultural-support-programme allocations, implementation and beneficiaries;
- input/seed/fertilizer supply and stock monitoring;
- agricultural financing and insurance;
- market-price regulation and monitoring;
- water resources, dams and climate-related indicators;
- fisheries/aquaculture, forestry/environment and food-security risk monitoring;
- a unified administrative-procedure portal.

Do not describe all these modules as operational. The official launch material makes clear that the first module is the harvest/threshing campaign and other functions are progressive future rollout. [S68]

**Research implications**
- The public platform may become a source of standards, data definitions, registries and sector-wide digital workflows.
- It may also reduce the opportunity for private products that simply replicate public registers or administrative portals.
- Private opportunities may instead emerge around farmer-facing workflows, equipment integration, agronomic services, data capture, farm management, supply chains, financing/insurance integrations and analytics—but each needs buyer and data-access evidence.
- No open API, third-party access policy, data-sharing terms or supplier procurement route has been confirmed.

### 2.2 Startup support and opportunity pipeline

The Algerian Agripreneurs programme publishes **35 Business Opportunity Sheets (BOS)**. Topics include quality-input logistics, poultry IoT, livestock modules, cold storage/transport, date sorting, milk testing, machinery rental, farm labour matching, farm advice/chatbots, crop monitoring, fertigation, digital certification, waste management, producer-export links and B2B producer/processor marketplaces. These are opportunity briefs designed to inform prospective innovators—not 35 operating startups, validated customer segments or proof of demand. [S69]

InnovAgro is part of the Development of Digital and Green Entrepreneurship in Algeria project and the EU agribusiness programme, co-financed by the European Union and German Federal Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development (BMZ), supported by the relevant Algerian ministry and implemented by GIZ. It offers strategic support/mentorship for agribusiness and agritech innovation. This is evidence of an institutional innovation pipeline, not a commercial market-size measure. [S70]

The Algeria Startup Challenge Foodtech page reports 185 registered projects, 24 selected projects and 9 winners. It lists projects in smart greenhouses, AI/IoT smart irrigation and phytopathology alerts, milk logistics, aquaculture, bee-tech and grain-stock management. Treat the counts and project status as programme-published data, not an audited census of active companies or deployed commercial products. [S75]

### 2.4 Agriculture workflows worth investigating

1. **Water/irrigation:** irrigation decisions, pumping energy, soil moisture, water access, maintenance and crop-specific schedules.
2. **Farm input distribution:** seed/fertilizer availability, quality, price, inventory, subsidy traceability and last-mile delivery.
3. **Crop-health response:** image-based diagnosis, expert validation, local pest/disease data, treatment recommendations, pesticide availability and follow-up outcomes.
4. **Harvest and post-harvest:** combine-harvester scheduling, harvest logistics, collection centres, storage, grading, cold chain and spoilage.
5. **Livestock:** animal identification, vaccination, feed, veterinary access and outbreak reporting.
6. **Food-chain traceability:** collection, processing, quality, transport, certification, producer/exporter matching and recalls.
7. **Farm economics:** input costs, yield estimates, financing, insurance, subsidy applications and market prices.

**Critical constraints:** fragmented farm sizes and crop types, rural connectivity, device/sensor capex, maintenance and calibration, seasonal demand, farmer trust, agricultural advice quality, language/literacy, service coverage and affordability. A technically strong sensor/app can fail commercially if installation, after-sales support or measurable return is weak.

### 2.5 What the agriculture evidence does not yet establish

- number of commercial farms willing to pay for software/sensors;
- installed-base and recurring revenue of local agritech companies;
- government SNAI API/data-sharing and supplier access;
- which crop/wilaya segments have sufficient farm concentration and unit economics;
- field-validated accuracy of AI disease identification or yield prediction;
- cost and reliability of drone, sensor, satellite and connectivity services;
- adoption/renewal and farmer willingness to pay;
- procurement budgets, awards and delivery for public agriculture IT.

## 3. Healthcare and medical administration

### 3.1 National digitalization programme and deployment stage

On 28 October 2026, the Ministry of Health said digitization is a strategic priority, and its digitalization director stated that broad digitization of all CHUs was targeted for **15 November 2026**. The same report said more than **2,355 health structures** had been connected to fibre, with an additional budget dedicated to digitization. It also described a national telemedicine system and teleradiology as introduced capabilities. [S78]

This is significant, but interpret the measurements carefully:
- fibre connection is infrastructure readiness, not proof an EHR is live or used;
- “digitization” may cover multiple operational systems, not one interoperable national longitudinal health record;
- the 15 November date is a stated target at the time of the announcement, not evidence of completion;
- the report says evaluation meetings were assessing implementation and rollout.

A separate APS report in April 2026 described a recently established digital platform for remote tracking of patient records intended to improve exchange of information between health structures. The article was tied to an announcement in Guelma; it does not demonstrate nationwide deployment or system-wide interoperability. [S79]

**Immediate event-driven follow-up:** after 15 November 2026, verify which CHUs actually completed deployment, which modules are live, whether clinicians use them in daily workflows, how many structures exchange records, which suppliers/system integrators are involved, and what procurement/acceptance records exist.

This market map shows that clinic/practice software is not an empty category. There are vendors with long operating histories and broad local functionality, including on-premise and bilingual workflows. A new general practice-management product would need substantial differentiation.

### 3.3 Healthcare workflows and opportunity hypotheses

Potential workflows to map separately:
- patient registration, identity matching and longitudinal record creation;
- referrals, appointments, admissions, discharge and inter-facility transfer;
- laboratory orders/results and imaging/PACS/RIS;
- medication, prescription, pharmacy stock and reimbursement;
- clinical coding, reporting and public-health registries;
- billing, insurance/third-party payer and Chifa-related administration;
- device/asset maintenance, procurement and hospital inventory;
- telemedicine and teleradiology;
- identity, consent, access logs, records retention and secure exchange.

Potential implementation/service opportunities—not validated product gaps:
1. migration and data-quality cleanup when facilities transition from legacy/local systems;
2. interoperability adapters and reconciliation between existing hospital, lab, imaging and pharmacy tools;
3. on-premise deployment, offline-first operation and resilient synchronization;
4. training, change management, helpdesk and local integration support;
5. privacy/security controls, access governance, auditability and backup/recovery;
6. operational analytics after trustworthy data capture exists.

### 3.4 Healthcare constraints

Health data is highly sensitive. Any product must meet legal requirements, least-privilege access, strong authentication, encryption, audit trails, incident response, retention/deletion policies and secure backups. Clinical features require domain validation and clear human accountability; an AI summarizer is not automatically a safe clinical decision-support system.

Other risks include long public procurement cycles, central-vs-facility authority, installed legacy software, device/vendor interoperability, clinical resistance to duplicate entry, network interruptions, uptime requirements, data migration risk and responsibility for inaccurate records.

### 3.5 What healthcare evidence does not yet establish

- completion of the 15 November CHU target;
- live EHR usage and facility-by-facility rollout;
- national interoperability architecture, terminology standards or data exchange APIs;
- public contract awards, software vendors, prices and service/acceptance results;
- current installed base and market share of private medical software;
- vendor security audits, certifications, hosting model and actual privacy controls;
- clinician satisfaction, workflow time savings, error rates or system uptime;
- whether public procurement permits new specialist vendors or relies on incumbent integrators.

## 4. Manufacturing and industrial operations

### 4.1 Adoption evidence

A 2025 study of Industry 5.0 adoption in Algerian manufacturing collected **47 questionnaire responses from 230 companies approached**. It concluded that adoption remained at an early stage and identified technology implementation barriers and future strategy as major impediments. The response count and method limit national generalization. [S86]

A separate 2025 study surveyed **102 Algerian subcontracting manufacturers** using an online structured questionnaire. It described gradual adoption of technologies such as e-commerce and supply-chain systems, with perceived operational-efficiency benefits but high initial investment and digital-skill shortages as barriers. [S87]

A June 2026 management-school thesis on SPEEDWIN, an aluminium/PVC joinery manufacturer, described a multi-system setup: WinPro for production, Logicom for sales/accounting and YT for marketing/commercial follow-up. The case involved interviews with five managers, direct observation and internal-document analysis. It is useful as a concrete example of fragmented point solutions, but one company cannot represent all Algerian manufacturing. [S88]

### 4.2 Industrial workflows worth mapping

- production planning, work orders, scheduling and capacity;
- inventory, procurement, supplier performance and traceability;
- quality control, defects, rework and non-conformance;
- preventive/predictive maintenance and spare parts;
- energy consumption, compressed air, water and waste;
- workforce skills, shifts, safety and production reporting;
- subcontracting, customer specifications, quotations and delivery;
- engineering changes, CAD/CAM and product lifecycle;
- finance/accounting, sales and production data reconciliation;
- industrial IoT, OT networks and cyber resilience.

The most plausible early opportunities may be operational integration and implementation rather than replacing full ERP/MES suites: connecting production, inventory, accounting and sales; quality/maintenance workflows; low-cost data capture; reporting and traceability; integration to machines and sensors; training/support for smaller factories.

### 4.3 Constraints

Capital availability, payback period, machine age, heterogeneous vendors/protocols, downtime risk, lack of internal IT/OT staff, digital skills, data quality, local service coverage, spare-part availability and cybersecurity segmentation all affect deployment economics. Industrial OT is safety- and availability-sensitive; do not apply generic IT automation patterns without industrial engineering and operational-safety expertise.

### 4.4 What manufacturing evidence does not yet establish

- a representative national adoption rate;
- the number of firms with ERP, MES, CMMS, WMS or industrial IoT systems;
- actual willingness to pay or budget by company size/subsector;
- which software products dominate in Algeria;
- machine/network integration costs and OT support capacity;
- quantified loss from downtime, scrap, quality defects or energy inefficiency;
- procurement volumes and vendor references.

## 5. Cross-sector interpretation

| Dimension | Agriculture | Healthcare | Manufacturing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public digitalization signal | SNAI launched with first module; other registries and workflows planned | CHU digitization deadline 15 Nov 2026; fibre, telemedicine and teleradiology reported | Policy and enterprise digitization pressure; no equivalent single national platform in this pass |
| Local provider field | Agritech startups and sensor/drone/advisory products | Established clinic/hospital/pharmacy software providers | Point solutions and integrators; map remains incomplete |
| Main data | Geospatial, crop, soil, weather, livestock, input and market data | Sensitive patient/clinical, imaging, lab, medication and operational records | Production, inventory, quality, machine, maintenance, energy and supply-chain data |
| Main deployment risk | Rural connectivity, seasonality, sensor maintenance, farmer affordability | Privacy, clinical safety, interoperability, legacy migration, uptime | Downtime, OT security, heterogeneous equipment, capex and integration |
| Plausible entry model | Field service + sensors/software, cooperative/channel, post-harvest workflow | Integration/migration/security/training, not generic EHR replacement | Vertical integration, quality/maintenance/energy workflow, implementation services |
| Current evidence gap | Farm-level ROI, paying users and real installations | Live rollout, procurement awards and actual interop | Current systems map, bottom-up segment counts and quantified losses |

### Cross-sector lessons

1. **Deployment is more important than announcement.** Track live use, data quality, user coverage, support and renewal.
2. **Local services and support are part of the product.** Rural sensor maintenance, clinical training and factory downtime support can dominate economics.
3. **Integration is often the real product.** Different systems already exist; a new UI without record exchange or process redesign may add another silo.
4. **Government platforms may be complements or competitors.** Identify which services the state supplies, which it procures, which data is accessible and which processes remain private.
5. **Sector-specific liability changes architecture.** Health data and industrial OT require stronger safety, privacy, security and assurance than ordinary consumer SaaS.
6. **A market can be technically underserved but commercially inaccessible.** Budget owner, procurement, channel, field deployment and support capacity must be mapped before building.

## 6. Next sector research questions

### Agriculture
- What is the actual production/usage status of each SNAI module after launch?
- Who owns data/API access and what are the public/private sharing rules?
- Which crop/wilaya segments have paid installations for Nabatic, AirCrop, Felatech and SIPTECH?
- What are sensor, drone, connectivity, support and agronomist costs per farm/season?
- Which support programmes have produced operating businesses, deployments and revenues—not just ideas or selected projects?
- Which farm problems have measurable, recurring economic loss and an identifiable payer?

### Healthcare
- After 15 November 2026, which CHUs completed digitization, which modules are live and how many users actively work in them?
- Who are the selected suppliers/integrators and what contracts, awards, prices and acceptance criteria exist?
- What standards, identifiers and interfaces enable EHR, PACS, LIS, pharmacy and telemedicine exchange?
- Which legacy systems will be maintained, migrated or replaced?
- What security, data-residency, access-control and audit requirements are contractually imposed?
- Which gaps do existing vendors acknowledge, and what is the cost of a new integration?

### Manufacturing
- Select two subsectors (for example food processing, plastics, metalworking, textiles, building materials) and map their value chains.
- Identify software, machine vendors, integrators and actual deployed systems.
- Quantify one recurring loss: downtime, scrap, rework, stockout, excess energy, delayed delivery or manual reporting.
- Determine whether a product, integration project, managed service or financing/lease model best fits the buyer.
- Validate the role of local ERP/MES/CMMS vendors and international systems already installed.

## 7. Current decision

**Keep agriculture, healthcare and manufacturing open as distinct research lanes. Do not commit to a broad vertical platform yet.**

Agriculture has visible startup/programme activity but weak evidence on commercial adoption and farm-level economics. Healthcare has a near-term public deployment deadline and a competitive private software market; the highest-value unknown is implementation/interoperability after the rollout target. Manufacturing shows gradual adoption and point-solution fragmentation, but there is not enough evidence to size or choose a niche.

The next best work is event-driven verification of the CHU deadline after 15 November, SNAI module status and data access, and a subsector-specific manufacturing workflow map with actual incumbent systems and costed operational problems.
