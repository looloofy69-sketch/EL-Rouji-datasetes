# Procurement, Digital Innovation Hubs and National Cybersecurity Strategy — New Signals

**Snapshot:** 2 November 2026  
**Status:** evidence update; no product or market-entry decision.

## 1. Executive assessment

This pass connects three evidence streams:
1. Algeria's National Information Systems Security Strategy 2025–2029 is accessible as a primary document and defines four strategic workstreams and sensitive sectors.
2. The first two Digital Innovation Hubs have proposed laureates: DIHMA for manufacturing in Algiers and Green Innovation Hub for renewable energy in Ouargla.
3. CERIST's 2026 procurement trail includes managed cybersecurity, anti-DDoS, backup/restore, GPU compute, SOC equipment and support for 5,500 existing Zimbra mailboxes.

The strongest inference is that implementation, maintenance, integration, assurance and workforce capability are recurring needs around existing systems. Buyer budgets, winning providers, contract value and renewal economics remain unverified.

## 2. National cybersecurity strategy: the actual priorities

The Ministry of National Defence's Information Systems Security Agency published the first edition of the **National Information Systems Security Strategy 2025–2029** on 3 March 2026. The 10-page primary strategy describes a four-year, progressive and measurable approach. [S107]

Its vision is to strengthen prevention, detection and response to cyber incidents, support digital transformation and preserve national digital sovereignty.

The document specifically identifies sensitive sectors: government institutions, public administrative and financial organizations, public safety, energy, health, water resources, telecommunications, transport, and production/distribution chains for essential food products. This is a policy-priority map, not a procurement-budget ranking. [S107]

### Four strategic axes

| Axis | Official direction | Potential implementation areas to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Technical-operational capabilities | Protect national systems and critical infrastructure; strengthen prevention, detection and response. | SOC/SIEM/MDR, vulnerability management, incident response, resilience, backup/recovery, critical-infrastructure security. |
| 2. Legal, organizational and normative framework | Strengthen cybersecurity law/organization and establish national standards. | Risk management, governance, security policies, data classification, standards adoption, audit evidence, regulatory implementation. |
| 3. Training, R&D and awareness | Develop qualified cybersecurity personnel, promote research/innovation and cyber awareness. | Professional training, cyber ranges, applied research, university/industry collaboration, secure engineering. |
| 4. National and international cooperation | Share resources and cybersecurity information across public/private stakeholders and with ASSI; coordinate internationally. | Public-private incident exchange, sector collaboration, supplier ecosystem, joint R&D, threat information. |

The strategy emphasizes sovereignty, coordination, shared resources and measurable objectives. The listed implementation areas are our interpretation, not procurement commitments. [S107]

**Decision implications:** the cybersecurity buyer map should include private organizations in critical sectors, not only public bodies. But supplier trust, qualification, references, sovereignty and sector-specific requirements may form meaningful barriers. Actual ASSI guidance, implementing standards, procurement plans and sector deployments remain to be established.

## 3. Digital Innovation Hubs: final selection results

The DGA project, implemented by GIZ with Algeria's Ministry of Knowledge Economy, Startups and Micro-enterprises, published final selection results for the first two Digital Innovation Hubs. [S108]

| Category | Proposed laureate | Strategic reserve / next-ranked |
|---|---|---|
| Manufacturing, Algiers | **DIHMA — Digital Innovation Hub for Manufacturing Algeria** | ASIA (2nd), Innov4Industry (3rd) |
| Renewable energy, Ouargla | **Green Innovation Hub (GIH)** | ECOHUB (2nd), AFAQ (3rd) |

The results page says 11 consortiums participated in the bootcamp and 10 dossiers were eligible for final evaluation. Assessment considered service maturity, territorial anchoring, field evidence from DMA/IMA diagnostics, and business-model sustainability. The two proposed laureates are expected to sign formal support agreements and receive strategic training and study-tour support. [S108]

### Why this matters

The DIH model could be a **distribution and capability-development channel**: it may connect SMEs to diagnostics, technology providers, implementation partners, finance/support organizations and demonstration facilities.

However, the hubs are not customers by default. The public results page does not disclose full consortium membership, detailed service catalogues, budgets, funding allocation or signed agreement terms. Access to SMEs, procurement and paid pilots is not guaranteed.

### Follow-up

1. Obtain winning consortium membership and signed MoU.
2. Obtain each hub's final service portfolio and target SME segments.
3. Determine whether they provide diagnostics only or also implementation, finance access, demonstrations and vendor referrals.
4. Identify supplier selection, payment and performance evaluation mechanisms.
5. Determine whether independent software/integration providers can become delivery partners.
6. Track first SME assessments and resulting paid implementation projects.

### What the procurement trail indicates

- Security is procured as both managed services and infrastructure.
- Backup/restore procurement includes implementation, multi-year support and training, not merely software licensing.
- GPU hardware procurement does not prove a commercial GPU-cloud market or utilization.
- The Zimbra tender demonstrates recurring platform-maintenance demand.
- No award data means no reliable conclusion about supplier concentration, price or performance.

### Document-integrity warning

The 2026 managed-cybersecurity webpage links to a PDF whose extracted text ends with a 30 October 2024 deadline. The webpage describes a 12-month service, but that attachment should not be treated as the correct 2026 technical specification until CERIST confirms it. A third-party listing reports a 6 May 2026 deadline; that is a useful lead, not a substitute for the primary tender dossier. [S62, S63, S115]

## 5. Zimbra support and security lifecycle

CERIST's consultation identifies an existing Zimbra 10.1.16 installation and seeks one year of support and upgrade assistance for 5,500 mailboxes. [S110]

Zimbra published version **10.1.21 on 24 September 2026**. Its release notes list further security fixes, including stored XSS, WebDAV token/MFA handling, password-recovery weaknesses and OpenJDK security updates. This makes patch lifecycle relevant to support procurement. [S114]

This does **not** establish that CERIST is still operating an unpatched 10.1.16 instance on 2 November. The tender identifies the platform version at procurement time; it does not disclose current patch state, selected provider or whether a supported upgrade has already occurred.

**Commercial lesson:** support contracts can include configuration management, release/patch planning, tested upgrades, backup/restore, continuity and security evidence. Delivery would require appropriate platform expertise, authorized support, migration capability and an explicit liability model.
