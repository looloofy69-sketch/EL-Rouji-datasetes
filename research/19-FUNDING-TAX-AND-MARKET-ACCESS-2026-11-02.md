# Funding, Tax Incentives and Market Access in Algeria

**Research snapshot:** 2 November 2026  
**Purpose:** map how innovation work is funded, contracted, labelled and sold into large enterprises and public institutions.  
**Status:** commercial-access research; legal/accounting advice not provided.

## 1. Executive assessment

Algeria's startup ecosystem now has several mechanisms that may connect technology suppliers to actual buyers:

1. **Large-enterprise R&D/innovation obligation (Finance Law 2026, Article 119):** Algerian-law establishments with annual turnover of at least DZD 2 billion must allocate at least 1% of taxable profit to R&D or innovation, in-house or through eligible open-innovation partnerships.
2. **R&D/open-innovation tax allowance (Finance Law 2025, Article 147 quater):** qualified R&D and open-innovation spending may reduce taxable profit, subject to a 30%-of-accounting-profit ceiling and a DZD 200 million cap.
3. **Open Innovation platform:** companies can publish problems and labelled startups/incubators can propose solutions; official guidance describes a minimum six-month contract and required contract clauses for the allowance.
4. **Startup and Scale-up labels:** labels affect access to fiscal measures, public/private programmes and ASF investment. A label is a commercial-access credential, not proof of product quality or buyer demand.
5. **Algerian Startup Fund (ASF):** public equity/quasi-equity financing, investment support and partner introductions for labelled startups.
6. **Public procurement:** Article 41 of Law 23-12 creates a narrow direct-negotiation exception for labelled startups offering unique and innovative digital/innovation services. The last official status located for its implementing texts was a May 2026 statement that they were being finalized.

The combined effect could make **corporate open innovation and implementation partnerships** a meaningful route to market. But these mechanisms are conditional, paperwork-heavy and not yet evidence of completed spending or startup revenue. The next research step is to trace actual corporate challenges, contracts, tax claims, public procurement awards and funded deployments.

## 2. Two different R&D/innovation tax mechanisms — do not conflate them

### 2.1 Article 147 quater: tax allowance for eligible R&D and open innovation

Article 10 of Law No. 24-08 (Finance Law 2025) created Article 147 quater in the Code of Direct Taxes and Similar Taxes. It allows qualifying expenditure:
- on R&D carried out within the company; and
- on open-innovation programmes undertaken with companies holding the **Startup** or **Incubator** label,

to qualify for an allowance in determining taxable profit. The allowance corresponds to qualifying expenditure up to **30% of accounting profit**, subject to an overall ceiling of **DZD 200 million**. Article 11 of the same Finance Law repealed the previous Article 171. [S117]

This is a tax allowance, not a requirement to spend 1% of profit, not a direct grant and not automatically a cash refund.

### 2.2 Implementing order: the 2023 order was amended in 2025

The original interministerial order of 9 May 2023 defined eligible R&D and open-innovation spending. Article 5 requires the open-innovation contract to state:
- contract purpose;
- contract duration;
- expected deliverables and deadlines;
- rights and obligations of each party;
- monthly service amount and total contract value;
- allocation of intellectual-property rights;
- termination arrangements.

Article 4 requires eligible open-innovation spending to satisfy normal tax deductibility conditions and to be covered by a contract with a labelled startup or incubator for a minimum of **six months**. The order also requires supporting material such as the startup/incubator label, contract, invoices/contracts/agreements and other evidence. [S118]

Crucially, the interministerial order of **22 April 2025**, published in Official Journal No. 30, amended the 9 May 2023 order to implement Article 147 quater. It updates the legal reference from Article 171 to Article 147 quater and changes the wording from a deduction to an allowance for determining taxable profit. The six-month contract condition and Article 5 contract fields remain relevant in the amended framework. [S119]

### 2.3 Eligible spending categories described by the official Open Innovation guide

The platform guide groups in-house R&D expenditure into:
- operating costs of the R&D project;
- external services;
- intellectual property costs;
- homologation and standardization.

It describes open innovation as an arrangement in which a company contracts a labelled startup or incubator to carry out part or all of its R&D work. The guide reiterates the six-month minimum contract duration and the Article 5 contract clauses. [S120]

The detailed application and evidence requirements should be checked against the amended 2025 order and current DGI guidance—not only the older explanatory wording on the portal.

## 3. Article 119, Finance Law 2026: a separate mandatory R&D/innovation allocation

Article 119 of Law No. 25-17 (Finance Law 2026) creates a different mechanism: UNCTAD's investment-policy monitor independently summarizes the same obligation; the Official Journal text remains the controlling legal source. [S116, S121]

- It applies to establishments incorporated under Algerian law with annual turnover **equal to or above DZD 2 billion**.
- Each year, the establishment must allocate at least **1% of the taxable profit** of the financial year to R&D or innovation actions.
- The actions may be performed internally or through open-innovation programmes with labelled startups or incubators.
- Actions may be realized in the profit year or in the following financial year; when deferred, the company must file an undertaking with its annual results declaration.
- Failure to meet the required allocation gives rise to a tax calculated on taxable profit at a rate equal to the shortfall between 1% and the actual R&D/innovation-spend-to-taxable-profit ratio.
- The sectors concerned and modalities for applying the tax are to be determined by regulation. [S121]

**This is not Article 147 quater.** Article 119 is a mandatory annual allocation for qualifying large establishments, backed by a tax on the unspent shortfall. Article 147 quater is a tax allowance for eligible R&D/open-innovation expenditure.

### 3.1 The new declaration service is not yet live

The Innovation Ministry's R&D&I 2026 page says a new declaration service is **“opening soon”**. It lists:
- turnover threshold of DZD 2 billion;
- minimum allocation of 1% of taxable profit;
- annual digital declaration before 31 January;
- supporting documents determined by the nature of the action;
- a QR-secured electronic statement after processing;
- the ability to report actions carried out in the following financial year. [S122]

As of the 2 November 2026 research cutoff, the service is still described as forthcoming. The 31 January date is stated by the portal's service description; detailed implementing regulations and sector coverage still need verification.

### 3.2 Eligibility discrepancy: Scale-up versus Startup/Incubator

Article 119's statutory wording refers to open-innovation programmes with labelled **startups or incubators**. The Innovation Ministry's new R&D&I service page additionally lists labelled **scale-ups** as eligible partners. [S121–S122]

A Scale-up label application form exists, but a form is not a substitute for the law or implementing regulations. Until a regulation or official clarification resolves the discrepancy, do not assume a Scale-up is legally eligible for every Article 119 open-innovation allocation.

## 4. Open Innovation platform: an actual problem-submission route

The official Open Innovation platform has three distinct functions:
1. declare eligible in-house R&D expenditure;
2. declare open-innovation expenditure with a labelled startup/incubator;
3. allow a company to submit a problem/challenge for potential co-creation with startups.

The problem-submission form requests the company name, contact email and telephone, problem title, description, supporting file and consent to personal-data processing. This is a real lead/challenge intake interface, but it is not proof that a challenge has been posted, matched, contracted, completed or paid. [S123–S124]

The challenge page describes an intended matching workflow in which companies publish specific problems and startups propose solutions. It lists industries including manufacturing, agriculture, education, health, energy, transport, finance, retail, tourism, telecoms, cybersecurity and food/agro-industry. The page displays a last-update date of 21 December 2024, so its sector list should be treated as platform positioning rather than a current demand census. [S125]

### 4.1 Commercial implications

The platform could lower discovery costs between a large-enterprise buyer and a labelled startup, especially for:
- industrial digitalization, maintenance and production optimization;
- energy and resource efficiency;
- agriculture and food-chain operations;
- healthcare and data workflows;
- fintech, payments and cybersecurity;
- logistics, telecoms and enterprise data management.

A startup should not treat a submitted challenge as a qualified lead until it verifies:
- problem owner and budget holder;
- urgency and economic consequence;
- data/site/API access;
- decision and procurement process;
- success criteria and acceptance authority;
- whether the buyer is prepared to sign a funded contract.

## 5. Startup and Scale-up labels: access mechanisms and requirements

### 5.1 Startup label

Startup.dz states that an applicant must already have incorporated a company to obtain the Startup label; an unincorporated project can apply for the separate Innovative Project label. Its published Startup-label conditions include:
- company age no more than eight years;
- an innovative product/service-based business model;
- significant growth potential;
- annual turnover below the amount set by the national committee;
- at least 50% of share capital held by natural persons, approved investment funds or other labelled startups;
- no more than 250 employees. [S126]

The site describes the label as an institutional passport to state startup benefits. The current threshold and case-specific interpretation of eligibility should be checked with the labelling committee before relying on it.

### 5.2 Scale-up label

Startup.dz has a Scale-up label application form. The form asks whether the applicant already holds the Startup label and requests legal-company records, annual turnover and social accounts for 2023–2025, a 2026 forecast, R&D expenditure evidence, founder technical qualifications and any patents/IP or awards. It also asks for proof that the company spends 5% of revenue on R&D. These are form fields and evidence requests; the form alone does not establish a complete, published legal eligibility rule. [S127]

### 5.3 Fiscal advantages listed by DGI

The DGI page, updated 30 March 2026, lists fiscal advantages for labelled startups and incubators, including:
- Startup-label holders: IRG, IBS or IFU exemption for four years from label issuance, with a possible two-year extension upon label renewal.
- Incubator-label holders: IRG/IBS exemption for two years, renewable under the stated conditions.
- VAT exemption on specified equipment acquired directly for startup/incubator investment projects, with equipment eligibility requiring validation by the national labelling committee.
- Eligible startup equipment under the cited provision is subject to customs duty of 5%. [S128]

These are conditional benefits tied to the law, the label and eligible equipment. They are not blanket exemptions from all taxes, payroll obligations, customs charges or VAT on every purchase.

## 6. Algerian Startup Fund: equity, quasi-equity and partner access

The ASF describes itself as a public venture-capital vehicle established in partnership with six public banks. It says it invests in labelled startups through equity and quasi-equity and provides hands-on post-investment guidance, partner networks, go-to-market support and export-readiness support. It states that regional mechanisms can offer tickets up to DZD 150 million per project depending on needs and maturity; this is a maximum advertised ticket, not an average or guaranteed amount. [S129]

Its website displays the following self-reported key figures:
- 963 startups received;
- 445 funding applications processed;
- 20+ business sectors;
- 41 wilayas covered;
- DZD 1.3 billion in introductions to public/private partners to run pilots.

The DZD 1.3 billion figure is specifically described as partner introductions for pilots, **not** cash invested by ASF. These figures are fund-reported metrics and have not been independently audited in this research. [S129]

ASF states that the Startup label is a prerequisite for partnering with the fund. Its A-Venture partner offers mentorship, go-to-market, corporate/POC access and investor readiness. [S129]

**Implication:** label acquisition may materially improve access to capital and corporate pilots, but funding is discretionary and depends on product, market, business model, governance, maturity and investment terms. It is not a grant entitlement.

## 7. Public procurement: two distinct startup/SME access mechanisms

Law No. 23-12 contains **two separate mechanisms** that should not be conflated. The official text is in Official Journal No. 51. [S130]

### 7.1 Article 41 — exceptional direct negotiation for unique digital/innovation solutions

Article 41 lists seven exceptional cases where the contracting authority can use direct negotiated procurement. One case concerns promotion of **labelled startups** providing services in digitalization and innovation, provided the solutions are **unique and innovative**. Direct negotiation is an exceptional procedure, not a general startup preference and not an automatic contract. [S130]

On 5 May 2026, the Directorate General of the Budget reported that the implementing texts for Article 41 were being finalized. That is the latest official implementation-status statement located in this pass; no later implementing text was confirmed here. [S131]

**Scope clarification:** Article 41 contains several separate exceptional cases, and at least one has a sector-specific order: an interministerial order dated 9 October 2025 defines cultural/artistic operations under Article 41's first indent. That order does not implement the distinct labelled-startup digital/innovation exception. The DGB's May 2026 statement specifically concerns the startup-related texts. Do not generalize the existence or status of one Article 41 order to all seven cases. [S164, S131]

### 7.2 Article 58 — reservation of needs/markets for SMEs, TPEs and labelled startups

Article 58 creates a different route. When certain needs of the contracting authority **can be satisfied by small or very small enterprises or labelled startups**, or by companies employing the minimum prescribed proportion of workers with physical disabilities, the authority must reserve the corresponding markets **exclusively** for these eligible categories, unless a justified exception applies. Where the exception is used, the contracting authority must explain it in the market-project presentation report. [S130]

The article also provides that these needs may be organized through a separate specification or a lot in an allotment, within a limit of **20% maximum of public procurement**. This is a ceiling in the statutory wording, not a promise that 20% will be allocated, not a startup-only quota, and not proof of the amount available to any one supplier. The denominator, category split, operational calculation and current procedure need confirmation through implementing texts and actual tender documents. [S130]

**Commercial relevance:** Article 58 can potentially reach suitable routine and specialized goods/services—not only digital products that qualify as unique and innovative under Article 41. It may create smaller lots or separate specifications that are more accessible to eligible firms.

**Important limits:**
- The need must be one that can actually be met by the eligible category.
- Reservation remains subject to Law 23-12 and procurement principles.
- A supplier still needs the required technical, financial, professional, tax and administrative capacity.
- A contracting authority may invoke an exception, but the law requires it to justify that exception in the relevant report.
- The 20% wording is a maximum limit on the identified category of needs; it should not be modeled as guaranteed reserved revenue or an automatic share for startups.
- No actual 2026 procurement example applying Article 58 to a labelled startup has been verified in this research pass.

### 7.3 Transition from the 2015 regulatory framework

Articles 110–112 clarify why procurement notices and guidance may still refer to Presidential Decree 15-247 of 2015:
- **Article 110** preserves the 2015 decree for procedures where an invitation notice had been sent for publication or a consultation launched before Law 23-12 entered into force, and for execution of contracts notified before that date. Previously approved specifications continue until the relevant award procedure is completed, subject to the stated process for bringing them into conformity.
- **Article 112** abrogates provisions contrary to Law 23-12 but says regulatory provisions remain applicable until new implementing regulations are published, insofar as they are not contrary to the law. [S130]

This means that the old decree's continued appearance in procurement resources is not automatically proof that the new law is ignored. At the same time, not every old provision can be assumed valid: direct statutory conflicts are abrogated, and transition rules must be applied to the specific procedure and date.

The DGB's May 2026 statement refers specifically to finalizing Article 41 implementing texts. It does not establish the implementation status of every other article, including Article 58. [S131]

### 7.4 Market-access implications

| Route | Potential fit | What must be proven |
|---|---|---|
| Article 41 | A genuinely unique/innovative labelled-startup solution in digitalization or innovation. | Implementing procedure, uniqueness test, authority discretion, award and delivery practice. |
| Article 58 | A need that can be fulfilled by an SME/TPE or labelled startup, potentially as a separate lot/specification. | How eligibility is verified, how the 20% ceiling is calculated, which lots are reserved, and actual awards to eligible firms. |

Do not build a B2G forecast around either route until current rules, procurement documents, awards and payment/acceptance records are visible.

## 8. The commercial-access chain

A plausible path from research to a contracted deployment now looks like this:

1. **Identify a buyer/problem:** use sector research, the Open Innovation challenge surface, DIHs, integrators or professional channels.
2. **Qualify the economics:** identify budget owner, current cost, access to data/sites, measurable target and procurement authority.
3. **Match the legal vehicle:** ordinary private contract, Article 147 quater open-innovation contract, Article 119 qualifying large-company programme, or a public procurement route.
4. **Check eligibility:** Startup/Incubator label, Scale-up label if explicitly accepted by the relevant legal provision, sector approvals, security requirements and supplier registration.
5. **Contract correctly:** define deliverables, acceptance, payment schedule, IP ownership, data rights, security, confidentiality, service levels, liability and termination.
6. **Deliver and evidence:** retain technical and financial proof, acceptance records, invoices and performance evidence needed for the contract, tax filing, funder or public customer.
7. **Convert to renewal:** measure the value delivered and seek maintenance, expansion or repeat deployment.

This is a commercialization framework inferred from the identified mechanisms, not a guarantee that every route is open in each sector.

## 10. What this changes in the opportunity map

### Stronger hypotheses

1. **Enterprise open-innovation delivery:** a labelled startup could sell a defined R&D/innovation work package to a large Algerian company under a qualifying contract; Article 119 may increase buyer attention once regulations and reporting service go live.
2. **Enterprise problem discovery and matching:** the Open Innovation challenge portal and DIHs could improve access to named problem owners, but only if real challenges and contract conversion are visible.
3. **Startup funding and scale-up support:** ASF and A-Venture offer finance and partner introductions, subject to label and investment assessment.
4. **Public-sector specialist delivery:** Article 41 may support a narrow B2G route for unique labelled startup solutions after implementing rules are published.

### Weak or unproven assumptions

- that every company with turnover above DZD 2bn will choose startup partnerships instead of in-house R&D;
- that Article 119's new service is operational and the sector rules are finalized;
- that scale-ups are eligible in all cases merely because the portal lists them;
- that the Open Innovation platform has active buyer challenges or completed contracts;
- that ASF application/partner-introduction metrics translate into funded deals or customer revenue;
- that Article 41 is currently usable without the implementing texts;
- that tax incentives are sufficient to overcome procurement, integration, security and delivery risk.

## 11. Priority follow-up

1. Identify Algerian-law companies with turnover at/above DZD 2bn and determine sectors, taxable-profit scale and R&D capacity.
2. Search their annual reports, procurement plans, innovation pages and corporate challenge announcements for actual R&D/open-innovation partnerships.
3. Obtain the current DGI guidance/circular and the full amended 22 April 2025 order; verify treatment of expenses, labels, contracts and evidence.
4. Monitor the R&D&I declaration service launch and the implementing regulation for Article 119, including the final sector list and scale-up eligibility.
5. Inspect completed Open Innovation platform challenges and convert the platform from a theoretical route into an observable buyer/pipeline dataset.
6. Obtain full DIHMA/GIH consortium membership and assess whether their SME programmes produce paid implementation projects and external supplier opportunities.
7. Verify ASF current portfolio/investments and distinguish applications, approvals, deployed capital, pilot introductions and exits.
8. Search for publication of Article 41 implementation texts and actual public awards under the startup exception.
9. Compare the expected procurement/tax/compliance path against ordinary B2B contracts to determine the fastest commercially credible route.

## 12. Current decision

**Add enterprise open innovation and commercialization access to the central research map. Do not rely on tax incentives or government programmes as revenue until actual contract conversion is evidenced.**

The more promising strategic question is now: which recurring, expensive problems can be solved by a technically capable labelled supplier in a way that a buyer can fund, contract, deploy and renew?
